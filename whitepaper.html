The essentials

Cortex brings together sound sessions, guided breathing and quiet visuals. Five paths — Breathe, Focus, Unwind, Sleep and Energy — organise the experience around your intention. These names describe the purpose of a session, not a guaranteed biological outcome.

Research on a technique and research on a product

The publications cited here did not study this version of Cortex. We do not present the app as a clinically validated treatment, a neuroprotective intervention or a device that measures brain activity.

What you hear

Sessions use sine tones separated between the left and right channels, plus an optional harmonic background or noise. Each path has its own background timbre; this is sound design, not a therapeutic dose. Tone frequencies and levels follow the stages of the selected protocol. You can adjust tones and background volume separately.

A binaural beat is a perceptual phenomenon associated with presenting different tones to the two ears. This is distinct from evidence that the whole brain has shifted to a chosen frequency. Ingendoh and colleagues found inconsistent results in research on this kind of entrainment. [1]

Parameters are not measurements

The numbers in session details describe the generated sound. Cortex does not perform EEG, identify sleep stages or measure focus. Your post-session rating is a subjective record only.

Audio v2: sound and evidence

Revision 2 retains the IDs, durations and target frequency pairs of all 14 sessions. It revises levels, AM depth and transitions. Frequencies have not been reselected as purported effective doses. These are original sequences informed by research context, without a clinical trial of Cortex.

Breathe

Soft flowing air throughout each inhale and exhale, with silence during holds. Separate guide volume.

Balban’s trial included five minutes of daily practice for a month, including box breathing. It did not test Cortex audio. Our synthetic breath is a pacing guide, not an additional therapeutic stimulus.

Balban et al., 2023 · Fincham et al., 2023 · active control

Focus

Steady tones and a discreet harmonic background for a longer work session.

Lane studied 29 people for 30 minutes: 16/24 Hz beats produced better vigilance results than 1.5/4 Hz. This provides context for rhythm experiments, not validation of our frequencies, durations or creativity effects.

Lane et al., 1998 · Orozco Perez et al., 2020

Unwind

A warm, soft background, gentle tones and unhurried transitions.

Mallik and Russo compared music, auditory beats, their combination and noise over 24 minutes. Outcomes depended on the group and stimulus. Cortex does not reproduce that music; Parasym Pathway does not imply demonstrated vagus nerve stimulation.

Mallik & Russo, 2022

Sleep

A low, subdued background, a gentle rhythm and a long fade at the end.

Lee’s pilot involved 10 people, dynamic 0–3 Hz beats and a silent control. It does not validate our sequences or control of sleep stages. Background sound is optional; more noise does not mean better sleep.

Lee et al., 2024 · Basner et al., 2026 · Vincens et al., 2026

Energy

A brighter harmonic background and a more distinct rhythm with a smooth opening and ending.

Attokaren tested attention during an hour of 40 Hz audiovisual stimulation in 62 people. Wu found an EEG response without improvement on the tested functions after 30 minutes. Our five- and sixteen-minute sequences with sinusoidal AM are a different procedure, not a validated activation dose.

Attokaren et al., 2026 · Wu et al., 2025

Volume values describe the digital signal, not the dB SPL level in headphones. Equal RMS energy does not guarantee equal perceived loudness for different timbres. Changing duration in a programme or export creates a sequence variant not validated by the cited studies.

What we know about breathing

Fincham and colleagues’ meta-analysis reported promising findings for breathwork and self-reported stress, while noting limitations in study quality and variation between studies. [2]

A later trial with an active control did not find coherent breathing superior to the comparison breathing practice. This is a reason not to attribute unique, certain effects to one breathing ratio. [3]

In Cortex, 4–6 is a simple guide: 4 seconds in and 6 seconds out. It is not a diagnosis or an individual prescription. Patterns with breath holds are optional. If a rhythm feels uncomfortable, return to natural breathing.

The NHS recommends gentle, regular breathing without forcing its depth. That principle informs our exercise instructions. [4]

Visuals and stimulation

The default animation is a quiet decoration. In 4–6 and 4–7–8 practice, the form’s changing size guides inhalation and exhalation. Box breathing uses a square: a point travels up during inhalation, across during a hold, down during exhalation and across during the next hold. The movement does not represent a brain signal or track your actual breathing.

Rhythmic light is an experimental option in Focus and Energy. A uniform field fills the player and, when expanded, the available page area. Choose “Adapt to display” or “Follow the protocol exactly”. Adaptation caps the frequency at the browser’s measured capacity: about 20 Hz at 60 frames/s. This is a modified stimulus, not faithful 40 Hz. Exact mode preserves the parameters and suspends light only during stages the display cannot support. Here, 40 Hz requires about 120 frames/s. The player shows the applied and requested values; audio is unchanged. Frame diagnostics do not measure the physical screen. Reduced motion disables pulsation.

W3C describes limits on flashing as an accessibility requirement. Meeting an interface criterion is not medical certification or a guarantee of comfort for every person. [5]

An EEG response and a session outcome

Chan and colleagues tested a dedicated 40 Hz device in an Alzheimer’s pilot study. It does not validate phones or Cortex. Attokaren’s 2026 study found better attention on a task during an hour of stimulation. Wu and colleagues, meanwhile, found an EEG response without improvements in the tested cognitive functions after a single session. [6] [7] [8]

No effective light dose has been established for Cortex. The full neutral field varies sinusoidally in linear sRGB luminance: 0.04, 0.06 or 0.08 peak-to-peak around a mean of 0.06. After colour rounding, the maximum change remains below 0.085, below the 0.10 change in W3C’s general-flash definition. These are engineering limits, not medical dosing or a safety guarantee. No saturated red is used. Screen brightness percentages do not measure lux. Do not enable with epilepsy or sensitivity to flicker.

In exact mode, Gamma Lift retains 38 Hz light and a 40 Hz tone difference in its middle stage. Adaptation may lower the light frequency; the player shows the applied value. This is not a clinical, synchronised 40 Hz protocol. Matching Hz numbers alone does not establish benefit or synchronise physical stimuli.

Less light in the evening

Research on evening screen exposure supports reducing light before sleep. Sleep sessions start with a night visual. Choose a black screen and tap once to return; this does not lock the phone. The browser does not control physical display brightness or the entire system interface. [9]

Limits and considered use

Your experience can depend on preference, surroundings and circumstances. We do not promise a specific time to fall asleep, improved N3 sleep, anxiety treatment, altered brain metabolism or a guaranteed rise in energy. A frequency-band label alone does not establish these outcomes.

Start at a comfortable, low volume. Stop if you feel uncomfortable. Use Cortex somewhere you can safely give the session your attention. See the guide for practical instructions.
